## exchange rate

## Exchange Rate Conventions

- **Two exchange rates** are presented:
  1. **Euro‑dollar rate** quoted in the standard way (e.g., USD/EUR).
  2. A rate quoted **opposite** to the usual convention.

## Interpreting Rate Movements

- **Rate increase** → the **local currency appreciates** (you receive more foreign currency for each unit of the domestic currency).
- **Rate decrease** → the **local currency depreciates** (you receive less foreign currency for each unit of the domestic currency).

## Exchange Rates and Monetary Policy

### 1. Exchange‑Rate Quotation
- **Exchange rate** can be quoted **opposite to the normal market convention** (e.g., quoting how many units of currency A are needed for one unit of currency B, instead of the usual direction).

### 2. Definition of Exchange Rate
- It is **the price of one currency relative to another**. This price fluctuates in response to **monetary policies** implemented by different countries.

### 3. Policy‑Exchange‑Rate Framework
- A **framework** links **monetary policies across countries** to **exchange‑rate movements** and examines their **short‑run impact on real activity** (output, employment, etc.).

### 4. Adapted Macroeconomic Model
- The **traditional macroeconomic model** used for very short‑run analysis is **adapted to an open economy with a floating exchange rate**. This allows us to study how policy shocks affect both the domestic economy and the exchange rate.

### 5. Short‑Run Analysis Period
- The analysis focuses on the period **when nominal prices are assumed fixed**. During this interval, the **effects of monetary policy on output** can be isolated and examined without the confounding influence of price adjustments.

## net exports

## Net Exports

- **Definition**: Net exports represent the **difference between foreign demand for domestic goods and domestic demand for foreign goods**. In the goods‑market equilibrium, they appear as an additional term.

- **Relationship with Domestic Output and Income**: Net exports are a **decreasing function of domestic output and domestic income**. As domestic income rises, residents purchase more imported goods, reducing net exports.

## real exchange rate

## Real Exchange Rate and Net Exports

- **Real exchange rate**: measures how expensive *domestic* goods are relative to *foreign* goods.
- A **rise** in the real exchange rate makes domestic goods **more expensive** abroad, reducing their competitiveness.

### Impact on Net Exports
1. **Foreign income increases** → **Net exports** (exports minus imports) tend to **rise** because foreign consumers can buy more of the country's goods.
2. **Exports** specifically grow when foreign income rises, as foreign demand for domestic products expands.

### Causal Relationship
- The **real exchange rate** directly influences **net exports**: a higher real exchange rate (domestic goods become pricier) typically **lowers** net exports, while a lower rate **boosts** them.

*Key takeaway*: Monitoring foreign income trends and the real exchange rate is essential for understanding and forecasting changes in a nation's export performance.

## exchange rate equation

## Exchange Rate Determination

The **exchange rate** is equal to the expression derived from the **uncovered interest parity (UIP) condition**.

### uncovered interest parity condition

## Uncovered Interest Parity (UIP) Condition

The **uncovered interest parity condition** states that the expected return on domestic assets should equal the expected return on foreign assets when adjusted for expected changes in the exchange rate. Formally, it can be expressed as:

```
(1 + i_{domestic}) = (1 + i_{foreign}) \times \frac{E_t[S_{t+1}]}{S_t}
```

where:
- `i_{domestic}` is the domestic interest rate,
- `i_{foreign}` is the foreign interest rate,
- `S_t` is the spot exchange rate (domestic currency per unit of foreign currency) at time *t*, and
- `E_t[S_{t+1}]` is the expected spot exchange rate at time *t+1*.

This relationship implies that any deviation from parity will be arbitraged away by investors seeking higher expected returns.

---

## Need for an Additional Equation

When introducing a new **endogenous variable** into the model—such as an expectation term, a policy variable, or a market-clearing condition—**an extra equation is required** to uniquely determine the system's equilibrium. This additional equation typically captures:
1. **Behavioral relationships** (e.g., consumption or investment functions),
2. **Policy rules** (e.g., a monetary policy reaction function), or
3. **Market-clearing conditions** (e.g., goods or asset market equilibrium).

Including this supplementary equation ensures that the number of equations matches the number of endogenous variables, allowing the model to be solved consistently.

## expected return equilibrium

## Market Equilibrium and Asset Returns

- **Equilibrium Principle**: In a balanced market, assets that promise higher *expected returns* attract additional capital. This inflow continues until the expected returns are **equalized across all assets**.

- **Investor Behavior**: When an asset offers a superior expected return, investors **reallocate portfolios** toward that asset, seeking higher gains.

- **Risk Adjustment**: Real‑world markets incorporate **risk adjustments**, meaning that returns are compensated for the risk taken.

- **Factor Isolation**: If all other influencing factors are removed, the **returns must converge** to similar levels across different assets or markets.

## Expected Capital Gain

- **Expected capital gain** refers to the profit anticipated from the change in the price of an asset over time.

## Bond Position Strategy

1. **Short the foreign bond**
   - Take a large short position in the foreign bond, betting that its price will decline.

   - Simultaneously take a very long position in the U.S. bond, expecting its price to rise.

- By shorting the foreign bond and going long the U.S. bond, you aim to capture the **capital gain** that arises from the price differential between the two securities.

## uncovered interest parity

## Interest Rate Parity and Exchange Rate Expectations

- **When the domestic interest rate exceeds the international interest rate**:
  - The **current exchange rate** must be **above the expected future exchange rate**.
  - This ensures that the **expected depreciation** of the domestic currency **offsets the higher domestic interest rate**.

- **Conversely, when the domestic interest rate is lower**:
  - The **current exchange rate** is **depreciated** relative to its future level.
  - The currency is expected to **appreciate**, compensating for the interest rate differential.

*Key takeaway*: The relationship between **interest rate differentials** and **exchange rate movements** is governed by the need for expected currency depreciation or appreciation to balance out the return differences between domestic and foreign assets.

## Interest rate increase reduces aggregate demand via two channels: investment fall and exchange‑rate appreciation.

## Effects of an Interest‑Rate Increase on Aggregate Demand

- **Interest‑rate hikes** lower aggregate demand through two distinct channels:
  1. **Investment channel** – higher rates increase the cost of borrowing, causing **investment** to fall.
  2. **Exchange‑rate channel** – in an open economy, a higher domestic interest rate attracts foreign capital, **appreciating the exchange rate**. This makes exports more expensive and imports cheaper, leading to a decline in **net exports**.

- In a **closed economy**, only the **investment channel** operates because there is no foreign trade to affect exchange rates.
- In an **open economy**, both the **investment** and **exchange‑rate** channels work together, amplifying the contractionary impact of an interest‑rate increase on aggregate demand.

| Item | Description |
| --- | --- |
| closed economy | In a closed economy the interest‑rate channel operated only through reduced investment. |
| open economy | In an open economy a second channel appears: a higher interest rate raises the exchange rate, making exports more expensive and imports cheaper, so net exports fall. |

## interest rate

# Exchange Rate and Interest Rate Dynamics

## Core Principle
- **Increasing the domestic interest rate** while other countries keep theirs unchanged **forces the exchange rate to appreciate**.
- The **interest‑rate differential** creates an incentive for capital to flow into the higher‑yielding currency, leading to an **appreciation of that currency**.

## Economic Consequences
1. **Output Contraction** – The stronger currency makes exports more expensive, which can **reduce output** in the domestic economy.
2. **Capital‑Loss Expectation** – The higher interest‑rate differential is **offset by an expected capital loss** on the currency, as investors anticipate the appreciation to reverse over time.

## Monetary‑Policy Implications
- In an **open economy**, monetary policy is more potent than in a closed economy because the **exchange‑rate channel** adds an extra mechanism for influencing the real economy.
- The **precision** of this channel is built into the policy framework, allowing central banks to affect both **interest rates** and **exchange rates** simultaneously.

## Common Misconceptions
- **No effect on interest rates:** Incorrect. Changing the domestic rate directly alters the interest‑rate differential.
- **No impact on exchange rates:** Incorrect. The exchange rate responds to the differential.
- **No implication for exchange rates:** Incorrect. In an open economy, the currency **tends to appreciate** when domestic rates rise relative to foreign rates.

# Overview of Fiscal Policy and Exchange Rates

## Key Points

- **UAP** (Unrelated Accounting Principle) **has no connection** to government expenditure in this context.
- **Government expenditure** does not influence the outcomes we are analyzing; the **driving force is the financial markets**.
- **Expected returns** are a central factor in the analysis of currency movements.

## Fiscal Policy and Exchange Rates

1. **When fiscal policy impacts interest rates**
   - If a fiscal measure changes interest rates, it can subsequently affect exchange rates.
2. **When fiscal policy does not affect interest rates**
   - In scenarios where fiscal policy has no effect on interest rates, it **does not influence** exchange rates.

## Practical Observations

- **Expansionary fiscal packages** (e.g., increased government spending or tax cuts) tend to lead to **currency appreciation** in practice, reflecting market expectations of higher returns.
