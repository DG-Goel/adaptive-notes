openness

Opening an Economy

  • When an economy opens to trade, consumers can purchase goods produced domestically as well as goods imported from abroad. This creates a need to compare the relative prices of domestic and foreign products.

Real Exchange Rate

  • The real exchange rate measures the relative price of a basket of goods at home versus abroad, expressed in a common currency. It indicates how many units of foreign goods can be obtained for one unit of domestic goods after accounting for price differences.

real exchange rate

Real Exchange Rate and Trade Effects

  • Real appreciation occurs when the real exchange rate rises. This makes domestic goods more expensive relative to foreign goods.
  • Real depreciation occurs when the real exchange rate falls, making domestic goods cheaper relative to foreign goods.

Impact on U.S. Exports

  1. A rise in the real exchange rate means U.S. goods become more expensive abroad.
  2. Higher relative prices reduce foreign demand for U.S. exports, leading to a decline in U.S. export volumes.

Influence of Trading Partners

  • When an important trading partner experiences higher output and income, its overall consumption—including imports—increases.
  • This boost in demand raises the partner’s imports of foreign goods, thereby increasing demand for the partner’s exports.

Current Context: China

  • China’s reopening is generating a large domestic economic boom, which can affect global trade dynamics, including demand for U.S. exports.

openness in financial market

Openness in Financial Markets

Openness refers to the ability of investors to choose between domestic and foreign assets for their capital allocation.

Investment Decision Example

  1. Available returns:
    • Domestic asset: 10% return.
    • Foreign asset: 5% return.
  2. Initial wealth: $1.
  3. Exchange rate: 0.8 £ per $1.
  4. Investment choice:
    • Convert the $1 into pounds: $1 * 0.8 £/$ = 0.8 £.
    • Invest the 0.8 £ in the foreign asset that yields 5% (or the example uses a 10% return, yielding 0.8 £ * 1.1 = 0.88 £).

Result

  • After one period, the foreign investment grows to 0.8 £ × 1.1 = 0.88 £ (assuming a 10% return on the foreign asset).
  • This illustrates how the exchange rate and foreign return affect the final amount when capital is allocated abroad.

expected exchange rate movement

Currency Expectations and Investment Returns

  • Indifference to Currency Movement: If you anticipate that the dollar will appreciate against the pound by 5 %, you are effectively indifferent to the outcome.

  • Investment Mechanics:

    1. Invest $1 today.
    2. The investment yields dollars tomorrow.
    3. If the pound depreciates relative to the dollar, any gain from a UK bond can be nullified when you convert the proceeds back to dollars.

  • Equilibrium Return: In equilibrium, both U.S. and U.K. bond strategies deliver the same net 5 % return when currency effects are accounted for.

uncovered interest parity condition

Uncovered Interest Parity (UIP) Condition

Definition: The uncovered interest parity condition states that the difference between the nominal interest rates of two countries equals the expected change in the exchange rate between their currencies.

i_{domestic} - i_{foreign} = E[\Delta s]
  • i_{domestic} – nominal interest rate on a domestic‑currency asset.
  • i_{foreign} – nominal interest rate on a foreign‑currency asset.
  • E[\Delta s] – expected percentage change in the spot exchange rate (foreign currency per unit of domestic currency).

Intuition

  1. Arbitrage Argument: An investor should be indifferent between:
    • Investing in a domestic asset and earning i_{domestic}.
    • Converting domestic currency to foreign currency, investing abroad to earn i_{foreign}, and later converting back at the expected future exchange rate.
  2. If the expected exchange‑rate movement does not offset the interest‑rate differential, arbitrage opportunities would arise, forcing markets toward UIP.

Key Implications

  • Expectations Matter: UIP links interest‑rate differentials to expected exchange‑rate movements, not to actual future rates.
  • Risk Neutrality: The condition assumes investors are risk‑neutral and do not require a risk premium for holding foreign assets.
  • Short‑Run vs. Long‑Run: Empirically, UIP often fails in the short run due to risk premia, transaction costs, and market frictions, but it provides a useful benchmark for long‑run analysis.

Example

Suppose the U.S. interest rate is 4 % and the Eurozone rate is 2 %. If investors expect the euro to depreciate against the dollar by 2 % over the next year, then:

i_{US} - i_{EU} = 4% - 2% = 2%
E[Δs] = 2% (expected depreciation of euro)

The equality holds, indicating no arbitrage opportunity under UIP.

Note: The UIP condition is a cornerstone of international finance theory, underpinning models of exchange‑rate dynamics and capital flows.

interest parity condition

International Interest Rate Parity

  • Interest Rate Parity (IRP) states that in equilibrium the domestic and foreign interest rates, adjusted for the expected currency appreciation or depreciation, must provide the same expected return to investors.

Dollar Appreciation

  • An increase in the value of the dollar relative to another currency is reflected by the dollar price moving upward in the foreign exchange market.

This strategy allows a country to benefit from trade flows and export opportunities without immediately exposing its financial sector to external volatility or regulatory challenges.

imports and exports

Macroeconomic Models Overview

1. Imports and Exports

  • Imports: Goods and services purchased from foreign producers.
  • Exports: Domestically produced goods and services sold abroad.
  • The interaction of imports and exports determines a country's net export position, influencing aggregate demand.

2. The IS‑LM Model

  • The IS‑LM model links the goods market (IS curve) with the money market (LM curve).
  • It is used to analyze equilibrium output and interest rates under different fiscal and monetary policies.

3. Goods‑Market‑Only Model

  • A simplified version of the IS‑LM framework that focuses solely on the goods market (the IS curve).
  • It ignores the money market, making it useful for studying the impact of changes in aggregate demand without monetary considerations.

4. Domestic vs. Global Demand

  • Resident demand for goods: The total amount of goods that domestic households, firms, and the government wish to purchase.
  • World demand for domestically produced goods: The sum of resident demand plus the demand from foreign consumers (i.e., exports).
  • The difference between these two quantities is the net export component:
    1. Resident demand → consumption, investment, government spending.
    2. World demand → resident demand + exports.
    3. Net exports = exports – imports.

Understanding these distinctions is crucial for analyzing how external trade influences the overall equilibrium in macroeconomic models.

Domestic demand

Domestic Demand

Domestic demand represents the total spending within an economy and is composed of three main components:

  1. Household consumption – the goods and services purchased by households.
  2. Firm investment – expenditures by businesses on capital goods, such as machinery, equipment, and structures.
  3. Government expenditure – spending by government entities on public services, infrastructure, and other governmental activities.

The sum of these three elements gives the overall domestic demand:

Domestic Demand = Household Consumption + Firm Investment + Government Expenditure

demand for domestic goods

Demand for Domestic Goods

  • Domestic demand for goods is the total demand for goods produced within a country, excluding the portion of that demand that is satisfied by imported inputs.
  • A component of this demand originates from foreign buyers who purchase U.S. products; this is often referred to as foreign demand for domestically produced goods.
  • When calculating domestic demand, the exchange rate is applied to the demand figure because some inputs may be priced in foreign currencies and must be converted to the domestic currency.

foreign demand

International Trade: Exports and Imports

Exports

  • Definition: Exports are determined by foreign demand for domestically produced goods.
  • They are represented by the variable x.
  • In our model, exports are assumed to increase with foreign output (i.e., they are an increasing function of foreign production).

Imports

  • Definition: Imports are goods purchased from abroad.
  • Key relationships:
    1. Imports increase with domestic output – as the domestic economy expands, firms and consumers demand more foreign goods.
    2. Imports increase with the real exchange rate (ε) – a higher ε makes domestic goods relatively more expensive, prompting consumers to buy more abroad.
  • Implication: When ε rises, domestic goods become costlier, so consumption shifts toward foreign products.

Consumption and Investment

  • Both consumption and investment are modeled as increasing functions of output.
  • Higher output stimulates greater domestic spending and capital formation.

Summary of Core Concepts

  • Exports (x): Driven by foreign demand; rise with foreign output.
  • Imports: Rise with domestic output and with a higher real exchange rate (ε).
  • Real Exchange Rate (ε): An increase makes domestic goods more expensive relative to foreign goods, boosting import demand.
  • Consumption & Investment: Both grow as overall output expands.

Domestic demand adjustment

Demand Curve Adjustments and the ZZ Curve

Domestic vs. Total Demand

  • Subtracting imports from total demand yields domestic demand. This operation shifts the demand curve downward and makes it flatter, indicating a rotation rather than a simple parallel shift.

Effects of Removing Import Demand

  1. Downward shift: Eliminating import demand lowers the overall demand level.
  2. Slope change: The curve becomes flatter because higher income levels experience a larger reduction in demand.

Key Observations

  • After adjusting for imports, the demand curve is flatter.
  • Foreign output acts as a parameter in this curve, but it does not alter the slope of the domestic demand curve.

The ZZ Curve

  • The ZZ curve represents domestic equilibrium output.

multiplier

Economic Multipliers

  • Rising income leads to higher consumption, which in turn increases income again, creating a chain reaction of growth.
  • In an open economy, the multiplier effect is smaller because some of the increased spending leaks abroad.
  • A flatter slope of the aggregate expenditure curve implies a lower multiplier, as each additional unit of income generates less additional consumption.

trade balance zero point

Trade Balance and Domestic Demand

  • Trade balance is zero when exports equal imports.
  • Domestic demand for goods matches the demand for domestically produced goods at this point.

Equilibrium Output

  • A point of interest is identified before finding equilibrium output.

Trading Partners' Income

  • After establishing equilibrium, we consider the income of our main trading partners.

Net Export Function

  • The net export function is calculated as:
    (Exports – Imports) / Exchange Rate
  • This function is a decreasing function of output.
  • Consequently, net exports equal zero at the equilibrium level.

trade deficit

Trade Balance Concepts

  • Trade deficit: occurs when imports exceed exports.
  • Trade surplus: occurs when exports exceed imports.
  • Balanced trade: the point where exports equal imports.

Net Export and Domestic Output

  • The level of domestic output at which net export equals domestic input represents a balanced economy.
  • When net export is zero, the economy is neither importing nor exporting more than it produces.

Effects of a Trade Deficit

  • A trade deficit reduces the multiplier effect because part of the additional demand generated by higher income is spent on foreign goods rather than domestic production.
  • Policymakers typically dislike a trade deficit.

Policy Responses

  1. Make domestic goods cheaper relative to foreign goods (e.g., through subsidies or tax incentives).
  2. Adjust the exchange rate to improve competitiveness, which can increase net exports.

net export function

Net Export Function and Trade Balance

  • Upward Shift of Net Export Function: When export demand (Y* ) rises, the net export curve shifts upward.
  • Impact on Domestic Output: Higher domestic output can increase the trade deficit.
  • Result of an Upward Shift: An upward shift in the net export function can lead the economy to finish with a trade surplus.
  • Scenario Comparison:
    1. Export‑driven scenario – the trade balance moves opposite to the movement observed in a domestic‑driven scenario.
    2. Domestic‑driven scenario – changes in domestic output affect the trade balance in the reverse direction.

Key takeaway: The direction of the trade balance depends on whether the economy is driven by export demand or domestic output, with opposite effects in each case.
