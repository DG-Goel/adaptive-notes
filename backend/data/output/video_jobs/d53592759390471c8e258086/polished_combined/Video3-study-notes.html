Interest Rates, Monetary Policy, and Exchange Rates

Overview

Japan has maintained stable interest rates for three decades, but recent signals suggest the first hikes in a long time. Market participants anticipate that U.S. interest rates will fall relative to Japan, implying that U.S. monetary policy will loosen before other economies. This expectation can generate large swings in exchange rates.

Key Relationships

  1. Domestic vs. Foreign Interest Rates
    • When the domestic interest rate rises relative to foreign rates, the exchange rate must appreciate.
    • The interest‑rate differential represents the compensation investors require for holding a currency with a higher rate.
  2. Monetary Policy Effects
    • Expansionary domestic monetary policy (e.g., lowering rates) typically leads to:
      • Contraction in output
      • Appreciation of the domestic currency
    • Conversely, a tightening policy (raising rates) can cause the opposite effects.
  3. Investor Expectations
    • When the domestic currency appreciates, investors foresee a potential loss on capital because the higher exchange rate reduces the future value of foreign‑denominated returns.

Summary of Implications

  • Higher domestic rates → currency appreciation
  • Expansionary policy → output contraction + currency appreciation
  • Interest‑rate differentials must be offset when rates change, influencing capital flows and exchange‑rate movements.

Exchange Rates, Monetary Policy, and Short‑Run Real Activity

Core Concepts

  • Exchange‑rate movements influence real economic activity in the short run.
  • Monetary policy differences across countries shape those exchange‑rate dynamics.

Analytical Framework

  1. Linkage of Policies to Rates – The framework connects monetary policies adopted in various economies to subsequent exchange‑rate fluctuations.
  2. Impact on Real Activity – It then examines how those fluctuations affect short‑run aggregate demand and output.

Theoretical Basis

  • The traditional IS‑LM model is applied to the very short run where nominal prices are fixed. This assumption isolates the effect of exchange‑rate changes on the real sector without the confounding influence of price adjustments.

Short‑Run Scenario

  • Assumption: Nominal prices remain constant.
  • Focus: Analyze how exchange‑rate dynamics immediately affect aggregate demand and output.
  • Outcome: Changes in the exchange rate shift the IS curve (through net exports) while the LM curve stays unchanged, leading to a new short‑run equilibrium for real activity.

Key Takeaways

  • In the short run, exchange‑rate movements act as a transmission channel for monetary policy differentials.
  • Holding nominal prices fixed allows a clear view of how exchange‑rate adjustments translate into changes in aggregate demand.
  • The IS‑LM framework remains a useful tool for understanding these immediate, real‑sector effects.

net exports

Net Exports and the Goods Market

Key Relationships

  • Net exports (NX) depend on three main factors:
    1. Domestic output (Y)
    2. Domestic income (Y_d)
    3. Imports (M)
  • As domestic output or domestic income rise, a larger share of demand is satisfied by foreign goods, causing NX to decline.
  • Conversely, when foreign (rest‑of‑the‑world) income rises, its demand for our goods increases, so NX increases.

Goods‑Market Equilibrium

  • The goods market is in equilibrium when output equals total demand:
    Y = C + I + G + NX
    where C, I, and G are domestic consumption, investment, and government spending, respectively.

Effects of Specific Variables

Domestic Output and Income

  • Higher domestic output → more domestic production, but also more imports; NX falls.
  • Higher domestic income → greater purchasing power, leading to higher imports; NX falls.

Real Exchange Rate

  • A higher real exchange rate makes domestic goods relatively more expensive than foreign goods.
    • Exports decrease.
    • Imports increase.
    • Net result: NX declines.

Foreign Income

  • When rest‑of‑the‑world income rises, foreign consumers demand more of our goods.
    • Exports rise.
    • NX increases.

Uncovered interest parity condition

Uncovered Interest Parity (UIP)

Uncovered interest parity is the condition that relates the expected change in exchange rates to the interest rate differential between two countries. It can be expressed as:

(1 + i_{domestic}) = (1 + i_{foreign}) * E_t[ S_{t+1} / S_t ]

where:

  • i_{domestic} = interest rate on domestic assets
  • i_{foreign} = interest rate on foreign assets
  • S_t = spot exchange rate (domestic currency per unit of foreign currency) at time t
  • E_t[ S_{t+1} ] = expected spot rate at time t+1

The parity implies that, when investors are risk‑neutral and there are no transaction costs, the return on a domestic asset should equal the expected return on a foreign asset after accounting for exchange‑rate movements. If the condition does not hold, arbitrage opportunities would arise, prompting capital flows that restore equilibrium.

Equalizing the Expected Rate of Return

Exchange Rate Determinants

  • The exchange rate is influenced by three key factors:
    1. The domestic interest rate
    2. The foreign (international) interest rate
    3. The expected future exchange rate

Impact of Domestic Interest Rate Changes

  • Higher domestic interest rate (ceteris paribus) leads to an appreciation of the domestic currency.
  • As the domestic currency strengthens, investors anticipate a loss on the currency component of any trade involving that currency.

Investor Behavior and Arbitrage

  • Traders will shift capital to the higher‑return U.S. dollar bond until the currency appreciates enough to neutralize the yield advantage.
  • This reallocation creates short‑lived arbitrage opportunities during the adjustment process, as market participants exploit temporary mispricings before equilibrium is restored.

expected exchange rate

Expected Exchange Rate and Its Effects

Key Concept: The expected exchange rate influences financial asset values and can shift various macroeconomic curves.

1. Impact on Asset Values

  • Expectations have a strong impact on financial asset values. When agents anticipate a change in the exchange rate, asset prices adjust accordingly.

2. Diagrammatic Implications

  • When the expected exchange rate rises:
    1. Identify which curves move in the diagram.
    2. The Liquidity‑Effect Model (LEM), controlled by the domestic central bank, does not shift with changes in expectations.
    3. The International Exchange Supply (IES) also remains unchanged when the expected exchange rate varies.

3. Determining Exchange‑Rate Movement

  • If equilibrium output stays the same after an increase in the expected exchange rate, the actual exchange rate (S) has not moved.
  • Conversely, a different output level signals that S has shifted.

4. Fiscal Policy Interaction

  • When the Fed permits fiscal expansion without altering the interest rate, the exchange rate remains unchanged.

5. General Trends

  • Rightward shifts in relevant curves typically indicate expansionary forces.
  • Declines occur when the exchange rate moves in a direction that reduces competitiveness.

Arbitrage

Arbitrage and Price Relationships

  • Arbitrage involves exploiting price differences across markets. When evaluating arbitrage arguments, always consider the counterfactual: what would happen if the expected price movement does not occur?

Present Value of an Asset

  • The current price of an asset must reflect its future price. In other words, an asset is valued today based on its expected future value.

Example: Expected Exchange Rate

  • Suppose the market expects an exchange rate of 1 euro per dollar in the next period. This expectation directly influences the current price of assets denominated in those currencies, as traders price them based on the anticipated rate.

positive relationship between exchange rate and domestic interest rate

Exchange Rate and Domestic Interest Rates

  • Positive Relationship: When the domestic interest rate rises, the exchange rate also rises. This indicates a direct, positive correlation between the two variables.

  • Equilibrium Condition: In equilibrium, market participants must be indifferent between holding domestic and foreign assets. Consequently, the exchange rate adjusts until this indifference is restored.

Uncovered interest parity

Interest Rate Parity and Exchange Rate Expectations

  • When the domestic interest rate exceeds the foreign (international) interest rate:

    • The spot exchange rate today must be higher than the expected future exchange rate.
    • This implies an expected depreciation of the domestic currency, which offsets the higher domestic interest rate.

  • When the domestic interest rate is lower than the foreign rate:

    • The spot exchange rate today is significantly depreciated.
    • An appreciation of the domestic currency is expected, compensating for the interest rate differential.

Key takeaway: The difference in interest rates between two countries is reflected in the expected movement of their exchange rates, ensuring that investors are indifferent between holding domestic or foreign bonds.

Open‑Economy IS‑LM (Mandel‑Fleming) Model

  • Unknown variables:
    • The output level (real GDP).
    • The domestic interest rate.
  • Given: One equation relating these variables, while all other quantities are treated as parameters.
  • Additional equation: The monetary‑policy condition, represented by the LM curve.
  • Result: With the IS curve (goods market) and the LM curve (money market) we obtain the complete IS‑LM model for an open economy, known as the Mandel‑Fleming model.

Effects of Interest Rate Changes on Aggregate Demand

Overview

An increase in the interest rate contracts aggregate demand through two main channels:

  1. Investment channel – higher rates make borrowing more expensive, so investment falls.
  2. Exchange‑rate channel – higher rates attract foreign capital, causing the domestic currency to appreciate. An appreciated currency makes exports relatively more expensive and imports cheaper, which lowers net exports.

Both effects reduce overall demand for domestically produced goods.

Detailed Mechanisms

1. Investment Reduction

  • When the cost of borrowing rises, firms postpone or cancel capital projects.
  • The decline in investment directly reduces aggregate demand.

2. Net‑Export Contraction

  • Appreciation of the domestic currency reduces the competitiveness of domestic goods abroad.
  • Consequently, exports fall while imports rise, shifting consumption toward foreign goods.
  • The net result is a decrease in net exports, further contracting aggregate demand.

Open‑Economy Considerations

  • In an open economy, the exchange‑rate channel can be the dominant driver of the contractionary impact.
  • The magnitude of this effect varies across countries:
    • United States: relatively insulated; the effect is modest.
    • Other economies: often more open and thus highly sensitive to exchange‑rate movements.

IS‑LM Framework

  • The IS curve depicts combinations of domestic output and domestic interest rate that satisfy goods‑market equilibrium.
  • In the presented IS‑LM model, net exports are incorporated, but the graphical shape of the IS curve remains unchanged.

Expansionary fiscal policy

Expansionary Fiscal Policy and Exchange Rates

  • Key Principle: An expansionary fiscal policy that does not alter the interest rate leaves the exchange rate unchanged.
  • When fiscal policy raises output while interest rates remain stable, there is no currency appreciation.
  • In an open economy, a rise in output from expansionary fiscal policy can lead to currency appreciation only if it influences interest rates.
  • If fiscal expansion causes investors to expect the central bank to raise interest rates, the currency tends to appreciate.

Consequence of Appreciation

  • Appreciation typically results in a decline in net exports.
