## openness

## International Trade and Capital Flows

- **International trade** involves an economy exchanging **goods** and **financial assets** with foreign economies. This requires comparing **relative prices** and **returns** across borders.
- The **relative price** of domestic goods versus foreign goods is expressed in a **common currency**, allowing for direct comparison of purchasing power and competitiveness.
- **Openness in the capital account** refers to the degree to which a country permits cross‑border flows of financial capital, influencing investment opportunities and risk exposure.

## real exchange rate

## Real Exchange Rate
- **Increase** in the real exchange rate → domestic goods become **more expensive** relative to foreign goods.
- **Decrease** in the real exchange rate → domestic goods become **cheaper** relative to foreign goods.
- It measures the **price of domestic goods** compared to foreign goods when both are expressed in a common currency.

## Interest Rate Parity (IRP)
- A condition that links **interest rate differentials** to **expected changes in exchange rates** without the need for forward contracts.

## Openness to Trade vs. Financial Openness
- **Trade openness**: Focuses on allowing the **international trade of goods** while setting aside the openness of financial markets.
- **Financial openness**: Refers to the **degree to which capital can move across borders**, influencing both **interest rates** and **exchange rates**.

## Example: Interest Rate Differential
- **UK bond** offers a **10 %** interest rate.
- **US bond** offers a **5 %** interest rate.
- The **10 % – 5 % = 5 %** differential illustrates how capital may flow to the higher‑yielding country, affecting exchange rates under the IRP condition.

## currency exposure

## Currency Exposure and Foreign Bond Returns

- **Currency exposure** can significantly impact the returns on foreign bond investments.

### Comparing Investment Options
1. **Determine the return** you expect from each investment.
2. **Adjust for exchange‑rate expectations** by dividing the foreign‑currency return by the projected exchange rate for the next year. This yields the return expressed in your home currency (e.g., dollars).

### Example: UK Bond vs. Domestic Bond
- **UK bond return:** 10% (bond yield)
- **Currency effect:** A 5% capital loss results from the appreciation of the dollar against the pound.
- **Net return:** 10% – 5% = **5%**

Thus, after accounting for currency movements, the UK bond provides a **net return of 5%**, comparable to the domestic alternative.

## uncovered interest parity condition

## Uncovered Interest Parity (UIP)

**Uncovered interest parity** is a fundamental concept in international finance. It describes the relationship between interest rates and expected exchange rate movements without the use of forward contracts.

## interest parity condition

## Interest Rate Parity in Equilibrium

In equilibrium, the **domestic interest rate** and the **foreign interest rate**, each adjusted for the **expected currency appreciation or depreciation**, are equal:

`i_{domestic} = i_{foreign} + expected\_exchange\_rate\_change`

This condition ensures that arbitrage opportunities are eliminated across currencies.

## distinction between demand for domestic goods and domestic demand for goods

## Open vs. Closed Economy Demand

- In a **closed economy**, the *demand for domestic goods* and the overall *domestic demand* are identical because there are no imports or exports.
- In an **open economy**, these two concepts diverge:
  1. **Domestic demand** remains the same as in the closed‑economy model.
  2. **Demand for domestically produced goods** is altered by the presence of **imports** and **exports**; part of the domestic demand is satisfied by imported goods.

Thus, while total domestic demand does not change when moving from a closed to an open economy, the composition of that demand shifts due to international trade.

## imports

# International Trade: Imports and Exports

## Key Relationships
- **Exports** are the counterpart of **imports**; they represent foreign demand for a home country's goods (i.e., what other countries count as their exports).
- **Exports decrease** when the **real exchange rate** appreciates (ε rises). An appreciation makes domestic goods relatively more expensive for foreign buyers, reducing foreign demand.
- **Imports increase** when:
  1. **Domestic output** rises – higher income leads to greater demand for foreign goods.
  2. The **real exchange rate** rises – a higher ε makes foreign goods cheaper relative to domestic ones, encouraging more foreign purchases.

## Summary
- An appreciation of the real exchange rate (higher ε) **reduces exports** and **boosts imports**.
- Growth in domestic output **raises import demand** regardless of exchange‑rate movements.

## DD curve

## Equilibrium and the DD Curve

- The **45-degree line** represents the equilibrium condition where **output equals demand**.
- The **DD curve** illustrates the **demand for domestically produced goods**.

## ZZ curve

## ZZ Curve and Export Effects

- **ZZ curve**: The adjusted demand curve that incorporates **imports and exports**. It is used to determine the **domestic equilibrium output**.
- **Exports** cause a **parallel shift** of the demand curve. This shift depends on **foreign output** and **does not alter the slope** of the curve.

## multiplier

## Multiplier Effect

The **multiplier** describes how an initial increase in income leads to higher consumption, which in turn generates additional income. This creates a chain reaction where each round of spending amplifies the original change.

### How It Works
1. **Initial increase** in income (e.g., through government spending or investment).
2. Households receive the extra income and increase their **consumption**.
3. The additional consumption becomes income for other producers and workers.
4. Those recipients again spend part of their new income, and the cycle continues.

The size of the multiplier depends on the **marginal propensity to consume** (`MPC`); a higher `MPC` means a larger multiplier effect.

## Open Economy Context

In an **open economy**, the multiplier operates alongside international trade flows. Exports and imports can amplify or dampen the domestic multiplier:
- **Exports** add to aggregate demand, reinforcing the multiplier.
- **Imports** leak spending abroad, reducing the domestic multiplier's impact.

Understanding the multiplier within an open economy helps policymakers gauge the broader effects of fiscal actions on both domestic output and the trade balance.

## trade balance

## Trade Balance and Equilibrium Output

- **Trade balance** occurs when a country's **exports equal imports**, resulting in **net exports of zero**.
- The **ZZ curve** represents the **demand for domestically produced goods** and is used to determine **short‑run equilibrium output**.
- The **trade balance** can also be expressed as the difference between the **demand for domestic goods** (the **DD curve**) and the **domestic demand for goods** (the **ZZ curve**). In formula terms:
  
  ```
  Net Exports = ZZ - DD
  ```
- When **ZZ = DD**, net exports are zero and the economy is at **equilibrium output**.

## equilibrium condition

## Equilibrium in an Open Economy

- **Equilibrium condition**: 
  ```
  y = C + I + G + (X - M)
  ```
  where `y` is output, `C` domestic consumption, `I` investment, `G` government spending, `X` exports, and `M` imports. In the notes this is simplified to **output (y) equals domestic demand plus exports minus imports**.

## Net Exports

- **Net export (NX)** is the difference between exports and imports:
  ```
  NX = X - M
  ```
- A **trade deficit** occurs when `NX` is negative, meaning the economy imports more than it exports. In this case, part of the additional income generated by higher expenditure is spent on foreign goods.

## Output Multiplier in an Open Economy

- The **output multiplier** measures how a change in autonomous spending affects total output.
- In an **open economy**, the multiplier is **smaller** than in a closed economy because a portion of the increased demand “leaks” to foreign goods (imports).

## Effects of Export Changes

- When **exports increase**, the **Z‑Z curve** (the aggregate supply curve in the open‑economy model) shifts **upward**, reflecting higher foreign demand for domestic output.

## International Linkages Example

- **China’s rising output** can be **caused by higher U.S. exports** to China. The increase in U.S. export demand boosts Chinese production, illustrating the interdependence of economies.

## Net exports

## Net Exports and Output Relationships

- **Net exports** rise when **autonomous foreign output** (`y*`) increases.
- They fall when **domestic output** rises, because higher domestic production leads to greater **imports**.

## assumption

# Economic Assumptions and Trade Balance

## Premise Statements
- An **assumption** is a premise taken to be true for the purpose of analysis.  
  - Example: *"We’ll assume that the second effect is not as strong as the volume effect."*
  - Example: *"Our assumption will be that net exports decrease when the currency appreciates."*

## Trade Balance and Exchange‑Rate Movements
- **Net exports** = **exports** – **imports**.
- When the **currency appreciates**:
  1. Domestic goods become relatively more expensive to foreign buyers.
  2. Foreign goods become relatively cheaper for domestic consumers.
  3. Consequently, **exports tend to fall** and **imports tend to rise**, so **net exports decrease**.
- When the **currency depreciates**:
  1. Domestic goods become cheaper for foreign buyers.
  2. Foreign goods become more expensive for domestic consumers.
  3. Hence, **exports tend to rise** and **imports tend to fall**, leading to an **increase in net exports**.

## net exports

## Trade Deficit

- **Definition**: A situation where a country's **imports exceed its exports**.
- **Suggested remedy**: The speaker proposes that the deficit can be reduced by **depreciating the exchange rate**, making exports cheaper for foreign buyers and imports more expensive domestically.

## depreciate exchange rate

## Exchange Rate Depreciation and Net Exports

- **Depreciating the exchange rate** shifts the **net export function upward**, improving the trade balance.
- This upward shift means that, for any given level of income, **net exports increase** because foreign goods become relatively more expensive and domestic goods cheaper for foreign buyers.

## Policy Implications

1. **Trade‑balance correction** often requires more than just boosting production; additional policy measures may be necessary.
2. While a **currency depreciation** can reduce a trade deficit, it is also **expansionary**, potentially increasing aggregate demand.
3. A common policy mix pairs **currency depreciation** with **reduced government expenditure** to offset the expansionary impact and keep overall demand in check.

## Key Takeaways

- **Depreciation** = upward shift of net exports → better trade balance.
- **Policy packages** often combine depreciation with fiscal tightening to balance growth and external stability.

# Macroeconomic Concepts

## Demand, Output, and Trade Balance

- An **increase in demand** for a good leads to a rise in **output**.
- When the **exchange rate depreciates**, domestic goods become cheaper for foreigners, improving the **trade balance** and boosting demand for domestic products.

## Government Expenditure and Net Exports

1. **Reducing government expenditure** can affect aggregate demand.
2. If the goal is **not to change output** but to **increase net exports**, consider strategies that raise exports.
   - **Export growth** improves the trade balance without altering overall output.

## Equilibrium Conditions

- **Net exports** are zero when the **DD curve** (demand for domestic goods) intersects the **ZZ curve** (aggregate supply).
