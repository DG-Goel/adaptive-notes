## exchange rate

## Exchange Rate Basics

- **Two exchange rates** are presented: one quoted as **euro‑dollar** (EUR/USD) and the other quoted in the opposite direction (USD/EUR), which is the reverse of the usual market convention.

## Interpretation of Exchange‑Rate Movements

- When an exchange rate **rises**, it indicates an **appreciation of the local currency** (in this context, the **dollar**). In other words, the dollar becomes more expensive relative to the foreign currency.

## Interest Rates and Currency Appreciation

- The dollar appreciates when the **domestic interest rate rises** relative to foreign rates. Higher domestic rates attract capital, increasing demand for the domestic currency.

## Interest‑Rate Parity (IRP)

1. If **domestic and foreign interest rates are equal**, the **expected future exchange rate** equals the **current exchange rate**.
2. Under this condition, investors anticipate **no capital gain or loss** from exchange‑rate movements.

## Relationship Between Interest‑Rate Differentials and Expected Exchange‑Rate Changes

- The core principle linking interest‑rate differentials to expected exchange‑rate changes is known as **interest‑rate parity**. It states that the difference between domestic and foreign interest rates should be reflected in the expected change in the exchange rate, ensuring no arbitrage opportunities.

# Exchange Rate, Interest Rates, and Equilibrium

## Core Principles
- **Only the exchange rate can adjust**; it moves until agents are **indifferent** again.
- The **domestic interest rate** is exogenously set (e.g., by the Fed, not the ECB).
- In equilibrium, **indifference is restored** by the exchange‑rate movement.

## Relationship Between Exchange Rate and Interest Rate
- The exchange rate and the interest rate have a **positive relationship**: when one rises, the other tends to rise.
- This link is formalized by the **Uncovered Interest Parity (UIP)** condition, which provides a point that ties the interest rate to the exchange rate.

## Determining the Exchange Rate
1. Plot the relevant curves (e.g., money market, goods market). 
2. Use the **interest rate** together with **UIP** to locate the equilibrium exchange rate.
3. The resulting point indicates where **both markets are in equilibrium**.

## Policy Implications
- **Policy actions** focus on the exchange rate and can influence **output** and the **interest rate**.
- The **interest rate** used in the analysis is the one consistent with equilibrium in financial markets (set by the Fed).

## Summary
- The exchange rate is the only variable that adjusts to maintain indifference.
- A positive correlation exists between the exchange rate and the domestic interest rate, captured by **UIP**.
- By combining diagrams with the interest rate and UIP, we can determine the equilibrium exchange rate and assess the impact on output and policy.

## net exports

# Net Exports

**Net exports** represent the difference between a country's exports and imports and serve as a component of aggregate demand.

## Key Relationships
- **Domestic output and income**: Net exports are a *decreasing* function of domestic output and domestic income. As domestic income rises, residents purchase more imported goods, which reduces net exports.
- **Foreign income**: An increase in foreign (rest‑of‑the‑world) income **raises** net exports because foreign demand for domestic goods grows.
- **Real exchange rate**: A higher real exchange rate **lowers** net exports, making domestic goods relatively more expensive to foreign buyers.

## Economic Intuition
1. Higher domestic income → higher imports → **lower net exports**.
2. Higher foreign income → higher foreign demand for domestic exports → **higher net exports**.
3. Real exchange rate appreciation → domestic goods become pricier abroad → **lower net exports**.

![net exports](visuals/visual-48c2b29d5423ca1b75c6.png)

## Foreign Income and Exchange Rates

- **Foreign income** influences the demand for a country's exports.

### Real Exchange Rate Effects

- When the **real exchange rate rises**:
  1. Domestic goods become relatively more expensive.
  2. **Net exports** fall because:
     - **Exports** decline as our goods are pricier for foreign buyers.
     - **Imports** increase as foreign goods become cheaper for domestic consumers.

### Modeling Considerations

- An additional equation is required in the model because the rise in the real exchange rate introduces an extra **endogenous variable**.
- The model assumes that both **domestic and foreign price levels are completely fixed**, isolating the impact of the exchange rate change.

**Uncovered interest parity** is the additional equation required when an endogenous variable—such as a **floating exchange rate**—is introduced into the model.

## Interest Rate Parity and Exchange Rates

- The **denominator** in the parity condition represents the **foreign interest rate**.
- By dividing both sides of the equation by the **exchange rate**, we simplify the relationship.
- This manipulation yields:
  
  `1 = (nominal exchange rate × expected appreciation of the currency) + …`
- When comparing the two sides, we must consider:
  1. The **domestic interest rate**.
  2. The **foreign interest rate**.
  3. The **expected appreciation** of the foreign currency.

In essence, the parity condition equates the domestic interest rate to the foreign interest rate **plus** the expected change in the exchange rate.

## expected exchange rate as a parameter

## Exchange Rate Expectations

- The **expected exchange rate** is treated as an *exogenous* parameter; it is not endogenized within the model.
- When market participants anticipate that the **dollar will appreciate** in the future, this expectation causes an **immediate appreciation** of the dollar today. In other words, raising the expected exchange rate leads to an instantaneous rise in the current exchange rate.
- By **fixing the expected exchange rate**, we prevent it from becoming an endogenous variable that could otherwise feed back into the model.

## arbitrage

## Arbitrage Relationships and Exchange Rates

- **Arbitrage‑type relationship**: Consider the scenario where arbitrage does **not** occur and examine the resulting market adjustments.
- The two bonds under comparison **pay the same interest rate**.
- One bond is expected to **appreciate by 10 %** relative to the other.
- **Investment strategy**: Short the foreign bond heavily while taking a large long position in the U.S. bond.
- When the **exchange rate rises**, the **domestic interest rate must increase**.
- There is a **positive relationship** between the **exchange rate** and the **domestic interest rate**.
- If the **domestic interest rate equals the international interest rate**, then the **exchange rate must equal the expected exchange rate**.

## Interest rate parity

## Interest Rate Parity and Exchange Rate Expectations

- **Interest rate differential**: When the **domestic interest rate** exceeds the **foreign (international) interest rate**, the **spot exchange rate** today must be **above the expected future exchange rate**. This creates an **expected depreciation** of the domestic currency, which offsets the higher return on domestic bonds relative to foreign bonds.

- **Conversely**, if the **domestic interest rate** is **lower** than the foreign rate, the **spot exchange rate** must be **undervalued (depreciated)** today. The currency is then expected to **appreciate** in the future, compensating investors for the lower return on domestic bonds.

### Key Takeaway
The relationship between interest rates and exchange rates ensures that expected currency movements **neutralize** the return differential between domestic and foreign bonds, maintaining parity across markets.

# Open Economy and Monetary Policy

## Key Points

- **Precision in policy**: The built‑in precision of monetary policy makes it more powerful than fiscal policy in a closed economy because it can influence the **export channel**.
- **Expansionary fiscal policy**: In an open economy, an expansionary fiscal stance **does not directly affect the exchange rate**.
- **Currency appreciation**: Generally, an open economy tends to see its currency **appreciate** when fiscal expansion occurs.
- **Interest‑rate adjustments**: Raising interest rates must be justified by a **domestic economic reason**.

## How Expansionary Fiscal Policy Leads to Appreciation

1. The treasury implements a **very expansionary** fiscal package, pushing output beyond the level consistent with a zero output gap or low inflation.
2. The central bank (e.g., the Fed) anticipates that such overheating will compel it to **raise interest rates**.
3. Higher domestic rates attract foreign capital, prompting a **conversion of exchanges** and causing the currency to **appreciate**.
4. Investors' expectations of the central‑bank reaction reinforce this appreciation.

## Summary

When a country adopts an expansionary fiscal package, the **expectation** that the central bank will respond by tightening monetary policy leads to **currency appreciation**. This dynamic underscores the interplay between fiscal actions, monetary responses, and exchange‑rate movements in an open economy.

# Economic Dynamics Overview

## Appreciation and Net Exports
- **Appreciation** of the domestic currency typically leads to a **decline in net exports**.

## Exchange‑Rate Expectations
- When the **expected exchange rate** shifts **rightward**, the **current exchange rate** must adjust by the same magnitude to prevent an anticipated capital gain or loss.
- This adjustment causes the **exchange‑rate curve** to shift **right**.

## Impact on Net Exports and Output
- Only **one curve** can move at a time. In this scenario, the **net‑exports curve** moves **leftward** because net exports fall.
- For any given **interest rate**, a leftward shift means **lower net exports**.
- Consequently, the **U.S. economy** experiences a leftward shift in its aggregate‑demand curve, leading to a **reduction in output**.

## Role of Monetary Policy
- If the **Fed** or the relevant **central bank** does **not** intervene, the described movements occur automatically.
- **No policy reaction** means the output decline persists; any active response could offset the shift.
