exchange rate

Exchange Rate Conventions

  • Two exchange rates are presented:
    • One quoted as euro‑dollar (EUR/USD).
    • The other quoted in the opposite direction (USD/EUR), which is less common.

Interpreting Movements

  1. Appreciation – When the quoted exchange rate increases, the local currency (the dollar) strengthens. This means you receive more foreign currency per unit of domestic currency.
  2. Depreciation – When the quoted exchange rate decreases, the local currency weakens, yielding less foreign currency per unit of domestic currency.

Note: The direction of appreciation or depreciation depends on which currency is the base in the quoted pair.

Interest Rate Policy and FX Movements

  • Perceptions of interest‑rate policy across regions are a primary driver of major foreign‑exchange (FX) fluctuations.
  • When U.S. monetary policy is tighter than that of other major economies, the U.S. dollar tends to appreciate relative to those currencies.

policy rate path

Policy Rate Path

Policy rate path refers to the trajectory that a central bank's policy interest rate follows over time, reflecting its monetary‑policy stance. Understanding this path helps analysts anticipate changes in borrowing costs, inflation dynamics, and overall economic conditions.

Mandela Fleming model

Monetary Policy, Exchange Rates, and Real Activity

  • Framework Overview: Connects monetary policies across countries to exchange‑rate movements and examines how these movements affect short‑run real activity.
  • Exchange Rate Definition: The price of one currency expressed in terms of another. The model studies how differences in monetary policy across nations shift the exchange rate and, consequently, influence aggregate demand.

net exports

Net Exports

Net exports are the component of aggregate demand that captures the difference between a country's exports and imports. In macroeconomic models, net exports are expressed as a function of output and domestic income.

Factors Influencing Net Exports

  1. Foreign (Rest‑of‑the‑World) Income

    • When foreign income rises, the demand for domestically produced goods increases.
    • Consequently, net exports increase.

  2. Real Exchange Rate

    • A higher real exchange rate makes domestic goods relatively more expensive compared to foreign goods.
    • This leads to lower exports and higher imports, causing net exports to decline.

Key takeaway: Net exports respond positively to foreign income growth and negatively to an appreciation of the real exchange rate.

nominal exchange rate

Real Exchange Rate Dynamics

  • Key Insight: When both domestic and foreign price levels are fixed, the nominal exchange rate (E) is the sole variable that can alter the real exchange rate (R).

    The relationship can be expressed as:

    R = \frac{E \times P_{foreign}}{P_{domestic}}

    With P_{foreign} and P_{domestic} held constant, any change in E directly translates into a change in R.

uncovered interest parity condition

Uncovered Interest Parity Condition

The statement refers to the uncovered interest parity (UIP) condition, which links interest rates across countries to expected exchange‑rate movements. In its basic form, UIP asserts that the difference between domestic and foreign interest rates equals the expected rate of depreciation of the domestic currency:

(1 + i_{domestic}) = (1 + i_{foreign}) \times \frac{E_t[S_{t+1}]}{S_t}

where i_{domestic} and i_{foreign} are the nominal interest rates, S_t is the current spot exchange rate (price of foreign currency in domestic terms), and E_t[S_{t+1}] is the expected future spot rate. This condition is fundamental in international finance and macroeconomics.

exchange rate appreciation

Exchange Rate and Domestic Interest Rate

  • Higher domestic interest rates cause the exchange rate to rise, meaning the domestic currency becomes more expensive.
  • An increase in the domestic interest rate leads to an appreciation of the exchange rate.
  • If the exchange rate is fixed and the currency appreciates today, it is expected to depreciate in the next period.

Interest rate parity

Interest Rate Differentials and Expected Exchange Rate Movements

  • Compensate the interest rate differential with an expected depreciation of the currency that carries the higher interest rate.
  • When setting the expected exchange rate, the only viable approach is to appreciate the currency today so that it can be expected to depreciate in the future.

Currency appreciation and return convergence

Currency Appreciation and Bond Returns

When traders shift their capital to a bond offering a higher return, the advantage is temporary. As the associated currency appreciates, the extra return is eroded, and the benefit disappears.

  • Key point: The profit from a higher‑yield bond is offset once the currency gains enough value.
  • Use currency appreciation to understand the diminishing returns on such trades.

expected exchange rate

Expected Exchange Rate

  • Expected exchange rate: The rate market participants anticipate for a currency pair in the future.
  • Expectations play a crucial role in the pricing of financial assets; they can drive market movements even before any actual change occurs.
  • When the expected exchange rate is revised upward, the current exchange rate typically rises immediately as traders adjust their positions to reflect the new outlook.

Uncovered interest parity

Interest Rate Parity and Exchange Rates

  • Domestic interest rate > International interest rate → The spot exchange rate today must be above the expected future exchange rate. The anticipated depreciation of the domestic currency offsets the higher domestic interest rate.
  • Domestic interest rate < International interest rate → The spot exchange rate is depressed today because the currency is expected to appreciate in the future, compensating for the lower domestic interest rate.

This relationship reflects the interest rate parity condition, where differences in interest rates are balanced by expected changes in exchange rates.

ISLM model in the open economy

Open Economy IS‑LM Model

  • IS‑LM model extended to the open economy context.
  • This framework is often referred to as the Mandel‑Fleming model.
  • The model adds an additional equation for monetary policy, represented by the LM curve.

Interest rate increase effect in open economy

Monetary Policy Effects

  • Higher interest rates act as a contractionary tool for two main reasons:
    1. Reduced investment – higher borrowing costs discourage firms from financing new projects.
    2. Appreciated exchange rate – a stronger domestic currency makes exports more expensive and imports cheaper, thereby lowering net exports.

International Trade and the IS‑LM Model

  • The IS‑LM model is extended to incorporate net exports (NX), allowing the model to plot equilibrium simultaneously in the financial market (LM) and the goods market (IS).

interest rate

Monetary Policy, Interest Rates, and Exchange Rates

Key Relationships

  • Interest Rate Determination: The Federal Reserve (Fed) sets the domestic interest rate, aiming for equilibrium in financial markets.
  • Uncovered Interest Parity (UIP): This condition links the domestic interest rate to the expected change in the exchange rate.

Effects of Changing the Domestic Interest Rate

  1. Increase in Domestic Interest Rate
    • The exchange rate must appreciate (the domestic currency strengthens) to satisfy UIP.
    • The appreciation reflects an expected capital loss on foreign‑currency assets, compensating investors for the higher domestic return.
  2. Expansionary Domestic Monetary Policy
    • Leads to a contraction in output.
    • Results in an appreciation of the currency.
    • The combined effect is more powerful than in a closed economy because the exchange‑rate channel influences net exports.

International Considerations

  • If the domestic interest rate moves while the rest of the world’s rates remain unchanged, the economy must compensate for the resulting interest‑rate differential.
  • The compensation occurs through an expected capital loss on the currency side: a larger appreciation without a corresponding change in expected exchange rates yields a greater loss for foreign investors.

Summary

  • The Fed’s rate decisions affect both output and the exchange rate.
  • UIP ensures that any interest‑rate differential is offset by expected exchange‑rate movements.
  • An expansionary policy (lower rates) typically weakens the currency, while a tightening policy (higher rates) strengthens it, influencing trade and capital flows.

monetary policy

Monetary Policy, Fiscal Policy, and Exchange Rates

1. Core Relationships

  • Monetary policy: The central bank raises interest rates to curb overheating output.
  • Interest rate impact: In the model presented, changes in monetary policy do not affect the exchange rate. The exchange rate remains unchanged unless fiscal policy influences the interest rate.

2. Fiscal Policy and Currency Movements

  • When a country implements expansionary fiscal packages, the currency often appreciates. This occurs because investors anticipate that the central bank (e.g., the Fed) will respond by raising interest rates.
  • If the Fed calls for fiscal expansion but keeps the interest rate unchanged, the exchange rate will not move.

3. Modeling Shocks

  • Increasing the expected exchange rate is treated as a shock in the model.
  • The Liquidity‑adjusted Exchange Model (LEM) is controlled by the domestic central bank.
  • The role of the Intertemporal Elasticity of Substitution (IES) is questioned within this framework.

4. Analytical Approach

  1. Fix a variable to isolate effects. For example, fix the interest rate when examining how other factors influence output and the exchange rate.
  2. Interpret output changes:
    • If output remains the same after adjusting the expected exchange rate, then the exchange rate itself (S) has moved.
    • If output changes, then the exchange rate has not moved; other mechanisms are at work.

5. Key Takeaways

  • Monetary policy influences interest rates but, in this context, does not directly move the exchange rate.
  • Fiscal expansion can lead to currency appreciation only when it triggers an interest‑rate response from the central bank.
  • Proper analysis requires holding one variable constant (e.g., the interest rate) to discern the true source of any observed changes in output or exchange rates.
