International Interest Rates and Exchange Rates

Key Points

  • Japan has not raised its interest rates for three decades.
  • The United States is expected to loosen monetary policy before other countries.

Interest Rate Changes and Exchange Rate Movements

  1. Domestic Rate Increase
    • When a country raises its interest rate while other nations keep theirs unchanged, the exchange rate tends to appreciate.
    • The appreciation compensates for the higher interest‑rate differential.
  2. Expected Capital Loss
    • Investors anticipate a capital loss on the currency to offset the higher return from the domestic interest rate.
    • This expected loss is the mechanism that balances the differential.

Monetary Policy Effects

  • An expansionary domestic monetary policy (i.e., lowering rates) typically leads to:

    • A contraction in output.
    • An appreciation of the currency.

  • Conversely, a contractionary policy (raising rates) can cause the opposite effects, depending on global rate movements.

Summary

  • Interest‑rate differentials drive exchange‑rate adjustments.
  • Appreciation occurs when a country's rates rise relative to the world, reflecting expected capital losses.
  • Monetary policy decisions in major economies, such as the U.S. and Japan, have significant spill‑over effects on global exchange rates.

The Mandela Fleming Model

The Mandela Fleming model links monetary policies across countries to movements in the exchange rate and examines how those movements affect real activity in the very short run.

Core Framework

  1. Short‑run IS‑LM: The model starts with the traditional IS‑LM framework, assuming nominal prices are fixed.
  2. Open‑economy extension: An exchange rate variable is added, allowing the model to capture international interactions.

How Monetary Policy Affects the Exchange Rate

  • Interest‑rate differential: When the domestic interest rate rises relative to the foreign interest rate, the price of foreign currency (i.e., the exchange rate) appreciates.
  • Bond return perspective: A higher domestic interest rate makes domestic assets more attractive, prompting capital inflows that push the domestic currency up.
  • Parity condition: If domestic and foreign interest rates are equal, the current exchange rate equals the expected future exchange rate. Raising the domestic rate then leads to an appreciation.

Key Implications

  • Aggregate demand: Changes in the exchange rate, driven by differing monetary policies, influence aggregate demand and output in the very short run.
  • Arbitrage assumption: The model assumes that arbitrage occurs instantaneously, so the exchange rate quickly aligns with expectations when policy changes.

Summary

  • The model demonstrates that monetary policy differences translate into exchange‑rate movements, which in turn affect real activity almost immediately.
  • Understanding the interest‑rate differential and the instantaneous arbitrage mechanism is essential for applying the Mandela Fleming model to open‑economy analysis.

Net Exports in the Goods‑Market Equilibrium

Overview

Net exports (NX) capture the difference between foreign demand for domestic goods and domestic demand for foreign goods. They are an essential component of the aggregate demand for goods and therefore appear in the goods‑market equilibrium condition.

Equilibrium Condition

The equilibrium condition equates total demand for goods—including domestic demand and net exports—with the economy’s output (Y):

Aggregate Demand (AD) = Domestic Demand + Net Exports = Y

Characteristics of Net Exports

  1. Dependence on Domestic Output
    • Net exports are a function of output that decreases as domestic output (and income) rises.
    • Higher domestic income raises the demand for imported goods, reducing net exports.
  2. Dependence on Foreign Income
    • Net exports increase when foreign (rest‑of‑the‑world) income rises.
    • Greater foreign income boosts demand for domestically produced goods.
  3. Effect of the Real Exchange Rate
    • A rise in the real exchange rate makes domestic goods relatively more expensive.
    • Consequently, exports fall and imports rise, leading to a decline in net exports.

Key Concepts

  • Domestic demand: Demand for goods produced within the domestic economy, distinct from overall demand that also includes foreign‑produced goods.
  • Foreign (rest‑of‑the‑world) income: Income earned abroad that influences foreign demand for domestic exports.
  • Real exchange rate: The price of domestic goods relative to foreign goods, adjusted for price levels.

By understanding how net exports respond to changes in domestic output, foreign income, and the real exchange rate, we can better analyze the dynamics of the goods‑market equilibrium.

Uncovered Interest Parity (UIP)

Uncovered interest parity is a condition that links the expected rate of return on assets denominated in different currencies. It states that, in equilibrium, the expected returns on comparable investments must be equal when expressed in a common currency.

Key Points

  • The UIP condition equalizes the expected return on a domestic asset with the expected return on a foreign asset after accounting for changes in the exchange rate.
  • In equilibrium, investors should be indifferent between holding a domestic asset or converting funds into a foreign asset, because the expected returns are similar in both places.

Formal Expression (for reference)

(1 + i_d) = (1 + i_f) * E_t[ S_{t+1} / S_t ]
  • i_d: domestic interest rate
  • i_f: foreign interest rate
  • S_t: spot exchange rate (domestic currency per unit of foreign currency) at time t
  • E_t[ S_{t+1} / S_t ]: expected rate of depreciation/appreciation of the foreign currency

Implications

  1. Arbitrage Opportunity: If the condition does not hold, arbitrageurs can profit by borrowing in the lower‑return currency and investing in the higher‑return one, adjusting for expected exchange‑rate changes.
  2. Exchange‑Rate Expectations: The condition implies that differences in interest rates reflect market expectations about future exchange‑rate movements.
  3. Risk Neutrality: UIP assumes investors are risk‑neutral and do not require a risk premium for holding foreign assets.

Practical Use

  • Economists and policymakers use UIP to forecast exchange‑rate movements based on interest‑rate differentials.
  • Traders may monitor deviations from UIP as potential signals of mispricing.

Interest rate parity

Interest Rate Parity and Exchange Rate Dynamics

  • Interest Rate Parity (IRP) states that when the domestic and foreign interest rates are equal, the spot exchange rate (S) equals the expected future exchange rate (E[S_{t+1}]).
  • If the foreign interest rate rises relative to the domestic rate, the domestic currency (e.g., the dollar) depreciates.
  • Conversely, when interest rates are identical across countries, the current exchange rate matches the expected future rate, leaving no arbitrage opportunity.

Arbitrage

Arbitrage and Exchange Rate Relationships

Core Concepts

  • Arbitrage‑type relationship: Anticipating a future price change and exploiting price differences. For example, expecting an exchange rate of 1 € per $ and then observing the market jump to that level.
  • The current price of an asset reflects market expectations about its future price.

Bond Example

  1. Two bonds are paying the same interest rate.
  2. One bond is expected to appreciate by 10 % relative to the other.
  3. Strategy:
    • Go short heavily on the foreign bond.
    • Go long heavily on the U.S. bond.

Equilibrium Condition

  • In equilibrium, investors must be indifferent between the two positions; otherwise arbitrage opportunities would persist.
  • The only variable that can adjust to restore equilibrium is the exchange rate.

Exchange Rate and Interest Rates

  • There is a positive correlation between the exchange rate and the domestic interest rate: as the domestic rate rises, the domestic currency tends to appreciate, affecting the exchange rate.

Impact of Higher Interest Rates on Aggregate Demand

  • Interest rate hikes contract aggregate demand through two main channels:
    1. Reduced domestic investment – higher borrowing costs discourage firms from investing, a classic contractionary effect.
    2. Appreciation of the exchange rate – a stronger domestic currency makes exports more expensive and imports cheaper, leading to a decline in net exports.

These mechanisms together lower overall demand in the economy.

Expansionary Fiscal Policy and Its Effects

Key Points

  • Expansionary fiscal policy involves either increasing government spending or reducing taxes.
  • According to the source, this type of policy does not directly change interest rates or exchange rates.
  • The exchange rate—the price of one currency in terms of another—remains unchanged unless the policy indirectly influences the interest rate.

Detailed Explanation

  1. Fiscal Actions
    • Government spending ↑ or taxes ↓ → aims to stimulate aggregate demand.
  2. Interest Rate Impact
    • The source asserts that expansionary fiscal policy does not affect the interest rate on its own.
  3. Exchange Rate Impact
    • Since the exchange rate is tied to interest rate differentials, no change in interest rates means no change in the exchange rate.
    • Only if the fiscal measures somehow alter interest rates would the exchange rate be affected.

Bottom Line

  • Expansionary fiscal policy → no direct effect on interest rates or exchange rates.
  • Any movement in the exchange rate would be secondary, occurring only through an interest‑rate channel.
